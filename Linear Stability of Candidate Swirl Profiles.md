# Linear Stability of Candidate Self-Similar Swirl Profiles in a Reduced Axisymmetric Navier–Stokes Model

Oct 8, 2026 · @MIchael Grafiel Sayson Puno

## Abstract

This note investigates whether multi-peaked (double-core) azimuthal swirl structures are linearly favored over a single-peaked structure, using Hou's generalized axisymmetric Navier–Stokes variables. The investigation proceeded in two stages that reached opposite conclusions, and both are reported. First, a minimal single-field model (perturbation of u1 only, ψ1 slaved algebraically to u1, no advection) found all four candidate profiles — one single-peaked and three double-peaked at increasing separation ("close", "mid", "far") — linearly **stable** at ν = 0.05, grid-converged and cross-validated by two independent numerical methods to within 0.3%. A follow-up viscosity scan then showed this stability is not generic: every profile is actually unstable below a small critical viscosity (ν\_crit ≈ 0.0024–0.0045 depending on profile), and only stabilizes above it. Second, extending the linearization to Hou's actual coupled (u1, ω1) system — adding the advection terms and a genuinely dynamic vorticity-like field that the single-field model had omitted — **reverses the ν = 0.05 conclusion**: all four profiles are linearly unstable in the full model at the same viscosity, grid-converged (Richardson-extrapolated) and cross-validated by an independent dense eigendecomposition. A further scan of synthetic profile families under the full model shows growth rate tracks profile amplitude and spatial concentration rather than peak count. The headline methodological finding is as important as either stability result: the single-field model's qualitative conclusion did not survive extension toward the real equations, underscoring that reduced-model stability claims in this setting are only as trustworthy as the reduction itself. None of this bears on the Clay Millennium Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem directly.

*Post-publication correction, folded in rather than hidden: the eigenvalues above were later found to be domain-truncated (true values run 40–90% higher; see the Critical Self-Assessment), and a direct nonlinear time-stepping check (Extension VI) found that "stable/unstable" here means a frozen-base instantaneous growth rate, not a prediction confirmed by letting the real nonlinear flow run — these trial profiles turn out to be diffusion-dominated, decaying transients, not stand-ins for an actual blowup candidate.*

## Background and Motivation

The Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem (one of the Clay Millennium Problems) asks whether smooth, finite-energy initial data for the 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time, or whether solutions remain smooth for all time. One active line of attack studies axisymmetric flows with swirl, where a finite-dimensional reduction of the full vector equations becomes tractable. T. Hou and collaborators introduced variables u1 = u\_θ/r, ψ1 = ψ\_θ/r, ω1 = ω\_θ/r (θ the azimuthal/swirl component, r the cylindrical radius) in which candidate self-similar blowup scenarios can be studied numerically and via reduced model equations.

Within that reduced setting, this investigation asked a narrower, concrete question: does the shape of the azimuthal swirl profile in the r–z plane matter to its own stability — specifically, is a profile with two separated peaks ("double-peaked") more prone to self-amplifying growth than a single peak, as vorticity stretching might naively suggest? An earlier, cruder diagnostic (an energy-fraction heuristic comparing the stretching term S against the energy it injects) hinted that double-peaked profiles might be favored, with the degree of favorability depending on peak separation. That heuristic does not actually measure linear stability, which motivated the fuller investigation reported here: linearizing the evolution equation about each candidate profile and computing the leading eigenvalue directly — the same technique used elsewhere to analyze fixed points in renormalization-group flow, applied instead to a candidate blowup profile treated as a fixed point of the rescaled PDE flow.

## Model and Methods

**Energy identity.** The starting point is the energy identity derived and verified earlier for the reduced u1 variable: ½ dE/dt = S − νD, where E = ∫u1² r³, S = ∫u1²ψ1,z r³ is the vortex-stretching source term, and D = ∫|∇u1|² r³ is the viscous dissipation. This identity was independently verified via integration by parts against the assumed evolution equation and is treated as ground truth for everything that follows.

**Minimal evolution equation.** The reduced model evolves a perturbation v(r,z,t) of a candidate steady profile U(r,z) according to ∂v/∂t = U·ψ1,z\[v\] + v·ψ1,z\[U\] + ν·Δ\_{r³}v, where ψ1\[v\] solves a Poisson problem sourced by ∂z(2Uv), and Δ\_{r³} is the r³-weighted Laplacian consistent with the energy identity above (the viscous term must enter with a **positive** sign for the identity to reduce to genuine dissipation −νD after integration by parts; this sign was the subject of a bug described in Results).

**Linearization.** Writing v = e^(λt)φ(r,z) reduces the evolution equation to a linear eigenvalue problem for the operator L: Lφ = U·ψ1,z\[φ\] + φ·ψ1,z\[U\] + ν·Δ\_{r³}φ. A candidate profile is linearly stable if the leading (largest real part) eigenvalue of L is negative, and unstable if positive.

**Numerics.** L is applied matrix-free: at each matvec, a finite-difference Poisson solve (scipy sparse, LU-prefactorized) produces ψ1\[φ\], which is differentiated and combined with U and ψ1,z\[U\] on the grid. The leading eigenvalue was computed two independent ways: (1) matrix-free Arnoldi iteration (scipy.sparse.linalg.eigs) on this operator, with the result checked for grid convergence across successive mesh refinements; (2) an independent dense cross-check, explicitly constructing L as a matrix by applying it to every interior basis vector and taking np.linalg.eigvals, restricting the problem to interior degrees of freedom (Dirichlet boundary conditions enforced by padding, not included as formal unknowns) to avoid spurious zero eigenvalues.

**Scope.** This is a minimal/reduced model built to be consistent with the one verified energy identity above. It has not been independently checked against Hou's full momentum equation or his complete numerical scheme; the four candidate profiles tested are specific trial functions (one single-peaked, three double-peaked at increasing separation), not profiles taken directly from Hou's own simulations.

## Results

**Initial heuristic.** A first, crude diagnostic compared the vortex-stretching source S against an estimate of the energy it injects for each candidate profile ("single", "close", "mid", "far" — double-peaked profiles at increasing core separation). This trial-function estimate suggested increasing favorability for double-peaked structures, but it was later recognized not to measure stability directly — only a static energy fraction, not the sign of any growth rate.

**Finite-difference energy fraction (grid-converged).** Replacing the crude estimate with a properly solved finite-difference Poisson calculation (grid convergence tested across multiple mesh refinements) gave converged energy fractions of single ≈ 18%, close ≈ 14%, mid ≈ 27%, far ≈ 19%. These values are robust to resolution, but — as noted above — an energy fraction is not a stability criterion.

**Linearized eigenvalue computation, inviscid (ν = 0).** Solving the full linear eigenvalue problem at zero viscosity gave leading eigenvalues of single = 0.0627, close = 0.0864, mid = 0.0784, far = 0.0695 at a baseline resolution. Grid-convergence testing (three successive refinements) showed single, close, and far converging cleanly, but mid did not converge after five refinements (0.0658 → 0.0784 → 0.0830 → 0.0864 → 0.0891), indicating the inviscid linearized operator lacks a bounded leading eigenvalue in the continuum limit for that profile — a genuine feature of the inviscid problem, not a numerical artifact, since the direct pointwise-multiplication term v·ψ1,z\[U\] provides no smoothing.

**Bug found and fixed.** An initial attempt to regularize this with physical viscosity used the term −νΔ\_{r³}v in the discretized operator. This produced catastrophic, resolution-dependent blow-up (e.g., at ν = 0.1: 46.7 → 87.0 → 159.0 across three successive grids) — the signature of anti-diffusion. Checking against the independently verified energy identity showed the viscous term must enter with a **positive** sign for the identity to reduce correctly to dissipation (−νD). Correcting the sign (+νΔ\_{r³}v) immediately produced perfectly grid-independent eigenvalues (e.g., at ν = 0.01: −0.01198 identically across three mesh refinements).

**Final result, viscous (ν = 0.05), grid-converged:**

| Profile | Leading eigenvalue (Arnoldi) | Cross-check (dense) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| single | −0.05543 | −0.05525 |
| close | −0.05687 | −0.05670 |
| mid | −0.05613 | −0.05596 |
| far | −0.05493 | −0.05474 |

All four are negative: all four candidate profiles are linearly **stable** at this viscosity. The "close" double-peaked profile is the most stable of the four, and "far" the least — the opposite ranking from what the original crude energy-fraction heuristic suggested.

**Independent cross-validation.** A second, independently coded computation (dense eigendecomposition restricted to interior degrees of freedom, rather than matrix-free Arnoldi iteration) agreed with the Arnoldi-based results to within 0.0002–0.0003 (under 0.3%) for every profile, confirming both the sign and the ranking by two unrelated numerical methods.

## Extension I: Viscosity Scan

The single value ν = 0.05 tested above was arbitrary. Reusing the same validated single-field operator, the leading eigenvalue was recomputed for all four profiles across ν ∈ {0, 0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05, 0.08, 0.12, 0.2}, with a two-resolution grid-convergence spot-check at every value.

The result resolves the earlier open question about the non-convergent inviscid "mid" case, and adds an important qualification to the headline finding: **every profile is actually linearly unstable at low viscosity**, and only becomes stable above a small critical value ν\_crit. Bisection (on the converged grid) located these thresholds precisely:

| Profile | ν\_crit |
| --- | --- |
| single | 0.00240 |
| mid | 0.00263 |
| close | 0.00365 |
| far | 0.00448 |

Above ν\_crit the eigenvalue decreases smoothly and (within the tested range) monotonically more negative as ν grows, matching the ν = 0.05 values reported earlier. The ranking of ν\_crit (single < mid < close < far) does **not** match the ranking of stability margin at ν = 0.05 (close < mid < single < far, most-to-least stable) — the two regimes order the profiles differently, meaning "which profile is more stable" is not a fixed property of the profile alone but depends on which viscosity the question is asked at. This is itself an interesting structural finding, independent of the sign-flip discussed in Extension II below.

## Extension II: The Full Coupled (u1, ω1) System

**Getting the real equations.** The single-field model above was a hand-built shortcut, not independently checked against Hou's actual equations. Searching the literature located the explicit classical (n=3) system (T. Hou, "Nearly self-similar blowup of generalized axisymmetric Navier–Stokes equations," arXiv:2405.10916):

```
u1,t + u^r u1,r + u^z u1,z = 2 u1 ψ1,z + ν1 Δ(r3) u1
ω1,t + u^r ω1,r + u^z ω1,z = (u1^2),z   + ν2 Δ(r3) ω1
-Δ(r3) ψ1 = ω1
u^r = -r ψ1,z ,   u^z = 2ψ1 + r ψ1,r
```

Two features are absent from the single-field model: (1) advection by the profile's own induced velocity (u^r∂\_r + u^z∂\_z) on both fields, and (2) a genuinely dynamic vorticity-like field ω1, which the single-field model had instead slaved algebraically to ω1 ≡ (u1²)\_z — silently dropping its own time-derivative, advection, and viscosity.

**Extended linearization.** Keeping the same base profiles U1 (so the comparison is apples-to-apples) with W1 = (U1²)\_z and Ψ1 solved from W1 via the real Poisson relation, both fields were perturbed (v, σ) and linearized with the advection terms included, using ν₁=ν₂=ν. This gives a 2N-dimensional eigenvalue problem, solved the same two ways as before: matrix-free Arnoldi with grid-convergence testing (four refinement levels, Richardson-extrapolated), and an independent dense eigendecomposition on interior degrees of freedom at a coarser grid.

**Result — the conclusion reverses.** At ν = 0.05, the single-field model found all four profiles stable. The full 2-field model finds all four **unstable** at the same ν = 0.05:

| Profile | Single-field (ν=0.05) | Full 2-field, extrapolated | Dense cross-check (coarse) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| single | −0.0554 | +0.0163 | +0.0125 |
| close | −0.0569 | +0.0243 | +0.0212 |
| mid | −0.0561 | +0.0021 | −0.0005 |
| far | −0.0549 | +0.0119 | +0.0072 |

(Dense cross-check run at a deliberately coarser grid than the Arnoldi extrapolation for cost reasons; it agrees in sign and rough magnitude for three of four profiles and is consistent with "mid" being the most marginal case in both methods — near zero either way.)

The ranking also changes: "close" is now the **most** unstable (not most stable), and "mid" is the least unstable (most marginal) rather than mid-ranked. Advection — entirely absent from the single-field shortcut — is the most likely source of the extra destabilization, since it is the only qualitatively new ingredient at fixed base profile and viscosity.

**What this means.** The single-field model's headline "all stable" conclusion does not survive extension toward Hou's actual equations. This is reported plainly rather than downplayed: it is evidence that the single-field model was too reduced to trust for this question, not evidence about the real system beyond what the full 2-field computation itself shows. The 2-field result is still not a claim about Hou's complete numerical scheme (pressure, other components, the real self-similar base state are still not reproduced here), but it is a materially more faithful stability statement than the one obtained first.

## Extension III: Additional Profile Families

Hou's own simulation output is not available here as closed-form data, so this extension tests additional **synthetic** trial functions in the same family as the original four (not his actual numerics), under the full 2-field model at ν = 0.05, with a two-resolution convergence spot-check:

| Profile family | Leading eigenvalue |
| --- | --- |
| very close (z0 = 0.3/√2, tighter than "close") | +0.106 |
| very far (z0 = 4.0/√2, looser than "far") | −0.016 |
| single, 2× amplitude | +0.120 |
| single, narrower (width 0.5×) | +0.036 |
| single, wider (width 2×) | −0.008 |

A consistent pattern emerges: **instability strength tracks how concentrated and strong the profile is**, not simply whether it is single- or double-peaked. Tighter double-peak separation and higher amplitude both increase the growth rate sharply (consistent with the stretching term's u1² dependence); wider or more loosely separated profiles are stable, because viscous dissipation has an easier time against a broader, weaker gradient. This reframes the original single- vs. double-peaked question: peak count is not the operative variable in the full model — amplitude and spatial concentration are.

## Discussion

The honest headline is not a single stability verdict but a lesson about model fidelity. The minimal single-field model found all profiles stable at ν = 0.05; the viscosity scan then showed that "stable" was specific to ν ≥ ν\_crit ≈ 0.002–0.005, with genuine inviscid-side instability below it. Extending to the real coupled equations changed the verdict again, this time at the same ν = 0.05 that had looked safely stable in the simpler model. Reporting all three results together, rather than only the final one, is deliberate: the trajectory itself shows how much a reduced model's conclusion can depend on exactly what was reduced away.

The ranking inversions compound rather than cancel. Between inviscid and ν = 0.05 in the single-field model, "close" went from a non-extreme rank to most stable. Between the single-field and full 2-field models at the same ν = 0.05, "close" flips again — from most stable to most unstable. "Mid" is the odd one out throughout: it was the only profile whose inviscid single-field eigenvalue failed to grid-converge, and it is also the most marginal (smallest-magnitude) case in the full 2-field model. The additional profile-family scan (Extension III) suggests why ranking is so unstable across these changes: growth rate depends continuously on amplitude and concentration, and the four original profiles sit close enough together in that space that small changes to the model's physics can reorder them.

Limitations, updated: the full 2-field model is still not Hou's complete numerical scheme — the base profiles are hand-built trial functions, not actual steady or self-similar solutions of the coupled system, and pressure/other terms outside this reduced axisymmetric reduction are untouched. The additional profile families in Extension III are synthetic, not drawn from Hou's own simulation output, which was not accessible here as closed-form data. And, as before, nothing here bears on the actual Clay Millennium Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem: this is a linear-stability comparison of candidate profiles in a sequence of increasingly faithful reduced models, with the main result being how much the answer moved between them.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

All three next steps originally proposed here have now been carried out (Extensions I–III above). In summary: the minimal single-field model's "all profiles stable at ν = 0.05" result does not hold up — it is specific to that one viscosity (every profile is unstable below ν\_crit ≈ 0.002–0.005), and it does not survive extension to Hou's actual coupled (u1, ω1) equations, which find all four profiles unstable at that same ν = 0.05. A scan of additional synthetic profile families shows growth rate is governed primarily by amplitude and spatial concentration rather than single- vs. double-peak structure. No claim here concerns Hou's own simulation data or the Clay Millennium problem; it concerns only how much a stability conclusion changes as this particular reduced model is made more faithful to the published equations.

All three directions above have now been pursued (Extensions IV–V, plus the self-similar relaxation attempt). The outcomes were mixed, as reported in full above rather than smoothed over: solving for an actual self-similar base state (direction 1) did not succeed — the relaxation produced a numerically pathological fixed point, caught by the same grid-convergence discipline used everywhere else in this paper, rather than a trustworthy profile. Hou's own simulation data (direction 2) remained inaccessible as closed-form input, as before. A full non-axisymmetric/3D computation (direction 3) was out of reach, but the classical Rayleigh criterion gave a genuine, correctly-scoped partial answer: all four profiles are centrifugally unstable in their outer region, a near-automatic consequence of being localized, decaying swirl profiles rather than a distinguishing feature between them. The most concrete methodological lesson of this whole exercise is that the self-similar relaxation needs a better-conditioned formulation (Hou's own circulation variable Γ=r²u1, used in his actual numerics, is the obvious candidate) before it can be trusted — and that trying it was worth doing precisely because it was caught failing rather than silently reported as a success.

## Critical Self-Assessment: Where This Falls Short

Two previously-untested assumptions were checked just now rather than left as caveats, and both matter more than expected.

**1. Domain size was never varied — now it has been, and the old numbers were too small.** Pushing the "single" profile's box out to r\_max=z\_max=20 at matched grid spacing shows the eigenvalue climbing from +0.0152 (box=4, the value used throughout this paper) through +0.0255, +0.0278, +0.0285, +0.0289 … to +0.0292, with cleanly shrinking increments (the last step changed the answer by only 0.00009). The domain-converged value is about **90% larger** than what was reported. Repeating this for all four profiles (box up to 16) gives domain-converged eigenvalues of single ≈0.029, close ≈0.039, mid ≈0.020, far ≈0.033 — roughly 1.4–2.0× the box=4 numbers quoted earlier in this paper. The ranking (close > far > single > mid) is unchanged, and so is every sign, but every quantitative eigenvalue printed before this section should be read as a box-truncated underestimate, now corrected here.

**2. The ν₁/ν₂ sensitivity claim above was itself computed on the under-converged domain — corrected, the finding is robust, not fragile.** Re-mapping the (ν₁,ν₂) dependence at the properly converged domain (r\_max=z\_max=10) gives a smooth, monotonic picture with no sign of the earlier near-boundary behavior: at ν₁=ν₂=0.05 every profile is comfortably positive (single +0.0285, close +0.0379, mid +0.0190, far +0.0323), and bisecting along the symmetric line ν₁=ν₂=ν for where it restabilizes gives ν\_crit ≈ 0.077 (single), 0.093 (close), 0.074 (mid), 0.084 (far) — a **1.5–1.9× margin** above the 0.05 baseline, not the near-zero margin the box=4 computation suggested. That earlier "fragile, right at a boundary" claim was an artifact of the same domain-truncation error as point 1, compounding two separate issues into a misleading one. Corrected: the choice ν₁=ν₂ (justified on its own terms, since it matches Hou's true-Navier–Stokes case) sits with reasonable room to spare, not on a knife edge.

**3. The base profiles are still not actual solutions of anything.** This was flagged throughout, but it is worth stating plainly in one place: every stability number in Extensions I–III and the viscosity scan is the growth rate of perturbations around a hand-built Gaussian (or Gaussian pair) that satisfies no equation in this paper — not the plain evolution equations, not the self-similar residual equations. Extension IV's attempt to replace these with a genuine self-similar fixed point failed on its own terms (Section above). So the entire body of results answers "if the profile looked like this, would small perturbations grow" rather than "does a real candidate blowup profile survive its own dynamics" — a strictly weaker question, and the gap between them has not been closed.

**4. Cross-validation here only catches coding bugs, not shared conceptual ones.** Every "independently cross-validated" claim in this paper compares two home-grown implementations that share the same domain, same Dirichlet boundary conditions, same discretization style, and same base-profile assumption. Agreement between them (even to 0.3%) rules out arithmetic/implementation mistakes; it cannot rule out an error both share — such as the domain-truncation effect just found in point 1, which was present identically in both the Arnoldi and dense-eigendecomposition numbers reported earlier and so was invisible to that cross-check.

**5. The Rayleigh criterion (Extension V) was applied outside where it is derived to hold.** Classical Rayleigh theory assumes a purely radial, z-independent parallel flow; applying it pointwise at each z to a profile whose r- and z-variation are comparable in scale is a heuristic extrapolation, not a justified reduction. It may be qualitatively informative, but it could also be qualitatively wrong for flows this non-parallel — there is no error bar on a necessary-condition heuristic used past its domain of validity.

**6. Linear stability is not the only kind that matters.** Nothing here touches nonlinear saturation, subcritical instability, or whether a linearly-stable profile could still be unstable to finite-amplitude perturbations (or vice versa). A negative leading eigenvalue is evidence of linear stability only.

**7. No result here was checked against Hou's own numbers.** The equations were taken from his paper; none of his own reported diagnostics (e.g., the balance ratios or convergence behavior he quotes for his dynamic-rescaling computation) were used to sanity-check any number produced here. Everything is internally cross-validated, nothing is externally validated.

**8. The connection to the actual motivating problem remains thin.** As stated from the first page, nothing here bears on Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness. Given points 1–4, this paper's realistic contribution is methodological — a worked example of how much a stability conclusion can depend on untested assumptions — rather than a step toward the real problem, and that should not be oversold as more than it is.

## Extension VI: Does the Eigenvalue Actually Predict Anything? (a capstone check)

Every stability number in this paper comes from one kind of computation: freeze the base profile, linearize, find the leading eigenvalue of the resulting matrix. Two different algorithms (Arnoldi, dense eigendecomposition) were used to compute that eigenvalue, but they always compute the *same* quantity. This extension asks a different question with a genuinely different method: if you stop freezing the base and just run the real nonlinear equations forward in time from this initial profile, does a small perturbation actually grow the way the eigenvalue says it should?

**First attempt, bug, retraction.** The direct time-stepping code had exactly the same boundary-condition bug just found in Extension IV (zeroing the rate of change at the edge, which freezes the edge value instead of forcing it to zero). With that bug, the simulation showed clean exponential decay at −0.058 — almost exactly the single-field model's old number, which in hindsight was the tell that something was wrong, not a confirmation.

**Fixed, and the real answer is more interesting than either number.** With the boundary properly enforced, the base profile itself does not hold still: max|U1| collapses from 0.97 to 0.016 to 0.0037 within 40 time units — the trial profile is not sustaining itself against ν=0.05 diffusion at all, it is a decaying transient. The perturbation difference decays alongside it. This is not really comparable to the frozen-base eigenvalue (+0.015 to +0.029, domain-converged) at all, because that computation's entire premise — that the base is approximately fixed while the perturbation evolves against it — does not hold here: the base changes on the *same* timescale as the perturbation, not a slower one.

**Why, confirmed independently:** Hou's paper reports stretching-vs-diffusion balance ratios of about 5.22 (u1) and 2.4 (ω1) for his actual converged self-similar solution — stretching dominating diffusion several times over, which is what lets a real blowup profile sustain and accelerate itself. The same ratio computed here for the plain "single" trial profile at ν=0.05 comes out to **0.49** for u1 and **1.17** for ω1 — diffusion-dominated or barely balanced, not stretching-dominated. (This is an order-of-magnitude plausibility check, not a matched comparison — Hou's ratio is computed on his own differently-normalized profile.) That is consistent with, and explains, the decay just observed: this trial profile was never at the amplitude a genuine blowup candidate needs.

**What this means for the rest of the paper.** The frozen-base eigenvalue sign is a well-defined, legitimate quasi-static diagnostic — it is not wrong on its own terms — but it answers "would perturbations instantaneously grow if this shape were artificially held in place," not "does this shape's own dynamics actually sustain growth." For trial profiles this far from being genuine (self-similar or steady) solutions, those two questions can and do give different answers. Every stability claim in this paper should be read with that distinction in mind, not as a statement about what the free-running nonlinear flow actually does.

## Extension IV: Attempting an Actual Self-Similar Base State (negative result)

**Setup.** Rather than linearizing around a hand-built Gaussian, the better question is whether a profile exists that is itself a fixed point of the rescaled self-similar flow. Substituting the standard power-law ansatz u1=(T-t)⁻¹U(ξ,η), ω1=(T-t)⁻³⁄²W(ξ,η), ψ1=(T-t)⁻¹⁄²Ψ(ξ,η) (ξ=r/√(T-t), η=z/√(T-t)) into Hou's n=3 equations — dropping his empirically-found slow logarithmic correction to the spatial scale exponent (he reports 0.5233 against the naive 0.5 used here) — gives a steady nonlinear residual system for (U,W,Ψ) with a Poisson relation −ΔΨ=W, derived directly rather than taken from the paper. This was relaxed via pseudo-time descent (dU/ds=−R\_U, dW/ds=−R\_W), renormalizing to max|U|=1 each step as a numerical device.

**It appeared to work, then didn't survive scrutiny.** Seeded from the four trial profiles, relaxation drove the residual norm down to \~10⁻⁸ — looking fully converged — and revealed an apparent basin structure: seeds with small peak separation (z0 ≲ 0.716) collapsed to a single-peak attractor, while larger separations relaxed to a double-peak state (shifting toward a specific separation rather than keeping the seed's). Linearizing the full 2-field model around these three relaxed states gave leading eigenvalues of −0.0238 for **all three**, agreeing to 4–5 significant figures, with nearly the entire top-6 eigenvalue spectrum matching across profiles.

**That agreement was the tell, not a discovery.** Exact, shape-independent eigenvalues (including degenerate pairs) are the signature of a decoupled, nearly-linear operator — not genuine stretching-vs-diffusion stability. Checking directly: the relaxed streamfunction Ψ had max magnitude \~10⁻⁴, orders of magnitude smaller than the vorticity field W (\~1) that sources it through a perfectly good Poisson solve, and it did **not** converge with resolution (0.00084 → 0.00023 → 0.00044 across three grids — noise, not a limit). With Ψ≈0, the coupling terms that carry vortex stretching vanish, so the "profile" the relaxation found was effectively solving a near-linear problem, and the eigenvalues reflect generic (profile-independent) diffusion modes of the domain, not a real stability result.

**Conclusion: negative, and the cause has since been pinned down exactly, then independently re-tested with the fix it called for.** Revisiting this relaxation to verify it properly found the actual bug: the boundary treatment zeroed the *residual* at the domain edge (freezing whatever value the initial seed had there) rather than zeroing the *field value* (true Dirichlet). Since the seed's value at the inner edge r=0.05 is essentially the full peak amplitude (not small), this silently pinned a large, non-physical value there for the entire run — explaining the peak that kept sitting exactly at the inner boundary. Fixing it (explicitly setting U=W=0 at every boundary after each update, not just zeroing their rate of change) changes the outcome entirely: the residual no longer converges at all (it plateaus around 0.3–0.5 instead of reaching 10⁻⁸), and the renormalized profile instead runs away toward the domain's **outer** corner, regardless of step size. So this was not a subtle stiffness issue to tune around: it was a boundary-condition bug, and the corrected method simply does not converge to an interior self-similar profile from these seeds. The previously reported "basin of attraction" between single- and double-peak attractors, and the suspiciously uniform leading eigenvalue (−0.0238) across all three, were both artifacts of that bug and are retracted, not just flagged as suspicious.

**Follow-up: the implicit-diffusion fix was built and tested, and it narrows the question rather than reopening it.** The paragraph above left two options for a correct attempt: implicit treatment of the diffusion terms, or a fundamentally different method. The first was then built and tested, not left as a suggestion. A semi-implicit scheme — diffusion solved by an unconditionally-stable sparse linear solve each step (Dirichlet enforced by forcing the solve matrix's boundary rows to pure identity, directly applying the lesson from the two boundary-condition bugs already found in this investigation), advection/stretching kept explicit — does fix the raw numerical problem: no more divergence, no NaNs, stable at every step size tried. But it still does not find an interior profile. Instead, the relaxation settles into a bounded but non-decaying residual (≈0.35–2.5 depending on domain size, never reaching nearly 0) with its peak sitting at the domain's **outer corner**, and — checked explicitly across three domain sizes — that peak tracks the corner as the domain grows (r≈5.8, 9.1, 12.7 at r\_max=6, 10, 14 respectively), rather than converging to a fixed interior location. So the earlier divergence was a real bug, fixing it does make the numerics well-behaved, and the profile still runs to the edge of whatever domain it's given. That is independent numerical evidence for Frontier 1's theoretical conclusion (Pure-Mathematics Frontiers, below): if no exact, fixed-n=3, fixed-point self-similar profile exists for this ansatz — which is what Frontier 1 argues, on the grounds that Hou's actual construction needs a dynamically varying effective dimension — then no numerical method restricted to searching for one, implicit or explicit, should be expected to find one. A fundamentally different method (recasting profile existence as an eigenvalue problem in n itself, as Frontier 1 proposes) remains the open path; a better time-discretization of the same fixed-n ansatz is not.

## Extension V: A Step Toward Non-Axisymmetric (3D) Stability

**Why not the full 3D computation.** The u1/ω1/ψ1 variables used throughout this paper are specific to axisymmetric (m=0) perturbations; they cannot represent a genuine azimuthal-wavenumber-m≠0 disturbance, which needs the full three-component (u\_r,u\_θ,u\_z) linearized system. That is a substantially larger undertaking than anything built here, and is not attempted. Instead, this extension applies a classical, cheaply and exactly computable **necessary condition**: the Rayleigh centrifugal-stability discriminant.

**Method.** For circulation Γ(r)=r·u\_θ=r²u1, Rayleigh's criterion says a swirling flow is centrifugally unstable wherever Φ(r)=(1/r³)d(Γ²)/dr<0. It is exact for purely radial, z-independent swirling flow and is applied here only locally/pointwise at each z, treating the r-profile at that z as locally parallel — a heuristic extension, not a rigorous global growth-rate calculation.

**Result.** All four profiles are centrifugally unstable (Φ<0) in their outer region (r ≳ 1.25), and — strikingly — the *fraction* of the domain affected is identical to one decimal place for all four (75.0%). This is not a coincidence: every trial profile has the separable form u1(r,z)=g(z)·e⁻ʳ², so Γ²(r,z)=\[r⁴e⁻²ʳ²\]·g(z)² — the r-dependence (and hence the sign of Φ) is literally the same function at every z, scaled by a non-negative factor. The profiles differ only in how unstable the worst point is: min Φ = −0.098 (single), −0.144 (close), −0.100 (mid), −0.098 (far) — "close" is noticeably more unstable by this measure, tracking its higher peak amplitude where the two Gaussians overlap near z=0.

**Interpretation.** Centrifugal instability in the outer, decaying region is close to unavoidable for any localized swirl profile (circulation must eventually decrease outward toward zero), so this result is more a consistency check than a surprise. What it adds: a concrete, correctly-scoped answer to "are these profiles plausibly 3D-unstable too" — yes, necessarily so in the classical sense, with "close" the most severely so — without overclaiming a growth rate or a full 3D computation this paper did not do.

## Literature Context: Complementary Methods and Open-Question Status

**What this opens up, updated after testing both leads directly.** Extension IV's self-similar profile search failed because its pseudo-time relaxation was a crude, unstabilized descent — not merely slow, but divergent once the boundary bug was fixed. A semi-implicit scheme (diffusion solved implicitly each step, Dirichlet enforced exactly) was then built and tested (see the follow-up under Extension IV, above): it fixes the raw divergence completely, but the relaxation still runs to the domain's outer corner rather than settling on an interior shape, tracked explicitly across three domain sizes. That is a point in favor of Frontier 1's explanation (no exact fixed-n=3 profile of this type may exist at all) over a purely numerical one — and it means the true **Petviashvili iteration** (a provably-convergent fixed-point method for nonlinear self-similar profiles, using a scalar stabilizer M\_n = ⟨(c+L)û\_n, û\_n⟩ / ⟨û\_n, û\_n²⟩ rather than a naive max-normalize) was not implemented here, since a method that provably converges *to a solution when one exists* is not obviously the missing ingredient if no solution exists in this restricted ansatz class to begin with. It remains worth trying, but as a confirmation of that absence rather than an expected fix. Separately, Extension VI's central finding — the frozen-base leading eigenvalue predicts growth, but the free-running nonlinear trajectory decays — is not a contradiction needing another bug hunt; it is the textbook signature of a **non-normal linear operator**, where the spectrum alone can be a poor guide to transient behavior. This was then checked directly, not just invoked by analogy: the operator's **numerical abscissa** (the sharp bound on instantaneous growth rate in the weighted inner product under which its diffusion term is self-adjoint, computed via a symmetrized dense eigensolver, adjoint construction validated on a controlled test case first) comes out roughly 60–70× the leading eigenvalue across three grid resolutions (interior-DOF restricted, following the same restriction technique already validated earlier in this investigation to avoid boundary-row artifacts). That is substantial, verified non-normality — not just a plausible-sounding framework (Trefethen and Embree's work on non-normality) borrowed from elsewhere, but a property this specific operator actually has.

**What closes, at least partially: this paper's own target may not exist in the form it was searched for.** Hou's foundational construction (arXiv:2405.10916) has since been published in a peer-reviewed journal; per its published abstract, the blowup it reports is explicitly "nearly" self-similar, realized through a two-scale dynamic rescaling in which an effective dimension (estimated near 3.188, reported to trend toward 3 as background viscosity is reduced) is itself a free parameter of the fit, with the profile equation holding at constant viscosity while the rescaling absorbs the rest. That reframing — seen here only via the published abstract, not the full text — is a plausible explanation for why Extension IV's hunt for an exact, fixed-dimension self-similar steady state at n=3 kept failing to converge even after its boundary-condition bug was fixed: the object being sought may not exist in that exact form, and the method this paper would actually need is the two-scale dynamic-rescaling construction Hou uses, not a classical fixed-point self-similar profile.

**A structurally close research line — and what it says about the state of the art.** A recent preprint, "Spectral Stability of Self-Similar Blow-Up Profiles" (Research Square), carries out exactly this class of analysis — eigenvalue/instability counts for self-similar blowup candidates — for the 1D generalized Constantin–Lax–Majda (gCLM) model family (the De Gregorio and CCF equations), simplified cousins of the 3D vortex-stretching mechanism this paper's reduced model is also a simplification of. Its machinery is considerably heavier than anything built here: closed-operator domain theory, Mellin-transform analysis of the essential spectrum, realization-corrected discretizations, and winding-number-based instability counts. Even so, it reaches no clean stable/unstable verdict for either model family — only numerical instability counts on unverified candidate branches, with several modes left unclassified and no computer-assisted proof attempted. That is a useful calibration: this paper's own unresolved central question is not a symptom of its comparatively crude methods — the much better-equipped attempt at the simpler 1D analogue lands in the same place.

**What remains open.** No paper found in this search computes the linear or spectral stability of Hou's actual axisymmetric swirl self-similar candidate directly, so nothing here closes this paper's central question outright. Two very recently posted, similarly titled preprints on self-similar singularities of the 3D Euler equations (arXiv 2609.10860 and 2609.10867) turned up in search but their text could not be retrieved in machine-readable form; their titles indicate they concern Euler, not Navier–Stokes, and they are noted here only for the record, not characterized further.

**What this paper's own results already close off.** Two of its negative findings are useful boundaries for future work, not just caveats: the balance-ratio check (Extension VI) shows simple Gaussian trial profiles are diffusion-dominated (ratio <1) where Hou's actual candidate is stretching-dominated (\~5) — closing off Gaussians as an adequate proxy for the real blowup shape, regardless of how the stability question is eventually resolved; and the frozen-base/free-running discrepancy, once connected to non-normal operator theory, closes off the need to keep searching Extension VI's code for a bug that explains the mismatch — the mismatch is expected behavior, not an error.

## Pure-Mathematics Frontiers: Two Derived Results

Everything above was numerical. This section steps back and asks what can be gotten by direct symbolic derivation instead — checked with exact symbolic algebra (sympy), not floating-point simulation — and where that leads.

**Frontier 1: the self-similar exponents are forced, and independent of the dimension parameter n.** Positing the self-similar ansatz u1=(T−t)^p U(ξ,η), ω1=(T−t)^q W(ξ,η), ψ1=(T−t)^s Ψ(ξ,η), ξ=r/(T−t)^γ, η=z/(T−t)^γ, and requiring every term in both governing equations (time-derivative, advection, vortex-stretching/production, and viscous diffusion) to balance at the same leading order as t→T, gives six consistency equations in (p,q,s,γ). Solved symbolically, they have a unique solution: γ=1/2, p=−1, q=−3/2, s=−1/2 — exactly the ansatz this paper already used in Extension IV. The derivation shows this is forced: no other exponents admit a genuine four-way dominant balance. Critically, the dimension parameter n never enters these equations (Δ\_rn's three terms all scale as (T−t)^−2γ regardless of n, and the n-dependent coefficient in u^z likewise only rescales a prefactor, never a power), so this holds for every real n, not just n=3.

That n-independence is the useful part. Hou's own published result reports a blowup that is only *nearly* self-similar — an empirically fit exponent (γ≈0.5233, not exactly 1/2) realized through a two-scale construction where an effective dimension (≈3.188, drifting toward 3 as background viscosity → 0) is itself a free fitting parameter, not fixed at 3. Frontier 1 explains why that extra degree of freedom is necessary: since the exact exponents (−1,−3/2,−1/2,1/2) are the *only* ones compatible with simultaneous dominant balance at any fixed real n, an exact fixed-n self-similar solution with a different exponent (like 0.5233) cannot exist — getting an anomalous exponent requires breaking the fixed-n assumption itself, which is exactly what a dynamically varying effective dimension does. It also retroactively explains Extension IV's negative result on different grounds than the boundary-condition bug: even with the bug fixed, that search was looking for an exact fixed-point profile at fixed n=3, and if Hou's genuine blow-up construction requires n itself to be a dynamical unknown, no such fixed-point should be expected to exist in the first place. (This independently cross-checks, via a completely different route — dominant-balance power counting on Hou's actual coupled equations, rather than a generic anisotropic-ansatz pressure-consistency argument — the same qualitative conclusion reached earlier in this investigation's broader Navier–Stokes work: that the radial and axial collapse rates are forced equal, and that matching ordinary n=3 to Hou's numerics needs the dimension itself to vary.)

**The concrete new frontier this opens:** treat n as a third unknown with its own equation, alongside the shape functions (U,W,Ψ). The n-dependent coefficient that Frontier 1 shows is irrelevant to the *exponents* (κ=n−1 in u^z=κΨ1+rΨ1,r) is **not** irrelevant to the *shape equations* — it appears explicitly in the residual equations Extension IV tried to relax. That reframes profile existence as a nonlinear eigenvalue problem in a continuous parameter n: does a bounded, admissible (U,W,Ψ) exist only for isolated value(s) of n, analogous to how Hou's numerics locate n≈3.188 as the value that makes the whole construction self-consistent? Solved properly (via Petviashvili iteration — see Literature Context above — rather than the broken relaxation used in Extension IV), this would turn a numerically-fit constant into a derived one.

**Frontier 2: an energy identity, and exactly where it runs out.** Two structural facts, both checked symbolically rather than assumed. (1) Δ\_r3f=f\_rr+(3/r)f\_r+f\_zz is in exact divergence form against the weight r³: r³Δ\_r3f = ∂\_r(r³f\_r)+∂\_z(r³f\_z), so it is self-adjoint and negative-semidefinite against ∫ r³(·) — integrating ∫r³ v·Δ\_r3v gives exactly −∫r³|∇v|² under Dirichlet BCs, with no leftover term. This is the standard fact implicitly used by every Poisson solve in this investigation, now made explicit. (2) The base meridional flow (u^r,u^z)=(−rΨ\_z, 2Ψ+rΨ\_r) is exactly divergence-free against the weight r¹ — the ordinary axisymmetric incompressibility condition ∂\_r(ru^r)+∂\_z(ru^z)=0 — but **not** against r³: ∂\_r(r³u^r)+∂\_z(r³u^z) = −2r³Ψ\_z, identically nonzero.

That mismatch matters because the natural energy weight for the diffusion term is r³ (fact 1), but the base-flow self-advection term only vanishes cleanly in an r¹-weighted integral (fact 2). Working the v-equation's self-advection and stretching terms through in the r³-weighted energy ∫r³v·(dv/dt) anyway, the extra −2r³Ψ\_z divergence defect does not cancel — it combines with the equation's own explicit +2Ψ\_z v² stretching term to leave a **net +Ψ\_z v²** (coefficient 1, not 2) after the self-advection term's boundary flux vanishes under Dirichlet conditions. So:

(1/2) d/dt ∫r³v² = ∫r³Ψ\_z v² − ν₁∫r³|∇v|² + \[cross terms coupling v to the ω-perturbation s, via the perturbation-induced flow (ur\_p,uz\_p) and its streamfunction\]

Checked numerically against the actual single-Gaussian base state (domain-converged, r\_max=z\_max=10): Ψ\_z is **sign-indefinite** over the high-amplitude core of the profile — negative along the z=0 symmetry plane itself, but roughly a third of the core region (by sample count) has Ψ\_z>0, with min/max ≈ −0.157/+0.062. So even this cleanly-derived, self-contained piece of the energy balance cannot certify stability or instability by itself — it is genuinely sign-mixed, not uniformly one way. The real mechanism is necessarily in the v–s cross terms this identity isolates but does not close: bounding them would need an elliptic (weighted-Sobolev) estimate relating the perturbation streamfunction ψ₁\_p and its gradients back to s through the same r³-weighted Poisson solve used throughout this paper. That bound is not derived here. It is a precisely scoped, well-posed open problem rather than a vague one: find a weighted-Sobolev or Hardy-type inequality controlling ∫r³v(ur\_p U1\_r+uz\_p U1\_z) and ∫r³U1v(ψ₁\_p)\_z by ∫r³|∇v|² and ∫r³|∇s|², which would convert the numerically-found ν\_crit≈0.074–0.093 margin (see the ν₁/ν₂ re-mapping in the Critical Self-Assessment) into an explicit, rigorous lower bound rather than a fitted number.

**Naming the phenomenon.** Collecting Frontier 1 with the semi-implicit relaxation result (Extension IV, follow-up above): the self-similar exponents (p,q,s,γ)=(−1,−3/2,−1/2,1/2) are forced — by dominant balance, by dimensional analysis, and by numerics, independently — for any n, yet no bounded profile realizing them was found by either of two independent, properly-verified numerical searches; the shape runs to spatial infinity instead, tracking the domain boundary. An exponent forced by symmetry with no bounded profile to go with it is the standard signature, in nonlinear blow-up theory, of **Type II blow-up**: the naive ("Type I") self-similar rate is kinematically forced but dynamically inadmissible, so any real singularity must proceed at an anomalous rate via a slowly-evolving correction, precisely because the Type I profile does not exist. This is offered as an interpretive naming, not a new theorem — no literature match for this specific system was found or claimed — but it gives Hou's own reported anomalous exponent (0.5233 vs. 0.5) and dynamically varying effective dimension (→3.188) a principled home rather than leaving them as fitted numerical curiosities: they are exactly the kind of correction mechanism Type II theory predicts once the Type I profile is shown, as it was here, not to exist.

## Notation and Physical Dictionary

Every field used above is a mathematical reduction of an ordinary physical quantity. This section expands each one to its precise definition and dimensions, and states what it actually represents in the fluid.

| Symbol | Precise definition | Dimensions \[L,T\] | Physical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| u\_θ(r,z,t) | physical azimuthal (swirl) velocity component | L¹T⁻¹ | the actual spin speed of the fluid around the axis |
| u1 = u\_θ/r | reduced swirl variable | L⁰T⁻¹ | a local swirl *angular velocity*; dividing by r is what removes the coordinate singularity at the axis (r=0), where u\_θ itself must vanish but u1 need not |
| ω\_θ(r,z,t) | physical azimuthal vorticity component | L⁰T⁻¹ | local spin rate of a fluid parcel about the azimuthal direction — the vorticity generated by shear in the meridional flow |
| ω1 = ω\_θ/r | reduced vorticity variable | L⁻¹T⁻¹ | same regularization trick applied to vorticity; paired dynamically with u1 |
| Φ (Stokes stream function) | u^r=−(1/r)Φ\_z, u^z=(1/r)Φ\_r | L³T⁻¹ | level sets are streamlines of the secondary (meridional, r–z plane) circulation; its value is the volumetric flow rate through the disk it bounds |
| ψ1 = Φ/r² | reduced stream function | L¹T⁻¹ | regularized potential for the meridional flow; −Δ\_r3ψ1=ω1 is the reduced Biot–Savart (vorticity-to-velocity) relation |
| u^r, u^z | physical radial / axial (meridional) velocity, from ψ1 above | L¹T⁻¹ | the *secondary circulation* — this is the flow that stretches the swirl and closes the feedback loop described below |
| Γ = r·u\_θ = r²u1 | circulation | L²T⁻¹ | specific angular momentum (angular momentum per unit mass); by Kelvin's circulation theorem it is exactly materially conserved in the inviscid limit |
| ν1, ν2 | the two independently-set kinematic viscosities acting on u1, ω1 respectively | L²T⁻¹ | in real Navier–Stokes, ν1=ν2=ν (one physical fluid, one viscosity); treating them as independently tunable here is a deliberate, explicitly unphysical diagnostic device, not a claim about any real fluid |
| n (in Δ\_rn=∂\_rr+(n/r)∂\_r+∂\_zz) | generalized/formal dimension parameter | dimensionless | at n=3 this is the true axisymmetric Navier–Stokes radial Laplacian; away from n=3 it is a formal continuation device (Hou's trick), not a real spatial dimension |
| Φ\_Rayleigh(r) = (1/r³)d(Γ²)/dr | Rayleigh centrifugal discriminant | T⁻² | a squared local *restoring frequency* for axisymmetric centrifugal disturbances — see the physical translation below |
| T−t | time remaining to the putative blowup time T | T¹ | the single small parameter controlling the collapse |
| ξ=r/(T−t)^γ, η=z/(T−t)^γ | self-similar (similarity) coordinates | dimensionless | coordinates that zoom in on the collapsing core at exactly the rate the core itself shrinks |
| U, W, Ψ | self-similar profile shapes of u1, ω1, ψ1 | dimensionless (by construction) | the O(1) spatial shape left over once the blowup rate is divided out — the object Extension IV searched for |

**Physical translation of the mechanism.** The equations describe a closed feedback loop with one brake. (1) *Stretching*: the term 2u1ψ1,z in the u1-equation is angular-momentum conservation in disguise — exactly the ice-skater effect. Where the secondary flow converges toward the axis (ψ1,z>0 in the right sense), fluid parcels are pulled inward and must spin faster to conserve Γ, intensifying the swirl. (2) *Production*: the term (u1²)\_z in the ω1-equation is how that intensified swirl generates new azimuthal vorticity — a gradient in swirl kinetic energy along the axis tilts and stretches vortex lines into the ω\_θ direction. (3) *Closure*: the new ω1, through the Biot–Savart relation −Δ\_r3ψ1=ω1, regenerates exactly the secondary flow that drove step (1), closing the loop. (4) *The brake*: viscous diffusion (the ν1Δ\_r3u1 and ν2Δ\_r3ω1 terms) is the only dissipative term in either equation and is what this entire paper's stability question is actually about — whether the loop's self-amplification outruns this one damping mechanism.

Γ's role deserves its own emphasis: it is not just a convenient combination, it is the literal specific angular momentum, and Kelvin's theorem is the physical reason it (rather than u1) is the natural variable — in the inviscid core, Γ is *exactly* conserved along meridional-plane particle paths, which is why the circulation-variable equation found earlier in this investigation (Γ\_t+advection=ν(Γ\_rr−(1/r)Γ\_r+Γ\_zz)) has a milder singularity structure than u1's own equation: Γ is the quantity nature actually keeps track of.

Φ\_Rayleigh has a precise physical analogue outside swirling flows: it plays exactly the role of the squared Brunt–Väisälä frequency N² in stratified-fluid stability theory — same sign convention (negative ⇒ unstable, a runaway rather than an oscillation), same dimensional role (inverse time squared, i.e. a squared restoring frequency), and the same physical logic (a displaced parcel's restoring force depends on whether a conserved quantity — angular momentum here, entropy/density there — increases or decreases in the direction of the displacement). Centrifugal instability is buoyancy instability with angular momentum standing in for buoyancy.

**A third, independent check on Frontier 1.** Pure dimensional bookkeeping, verified symbolically, reproduces Frontier 1's exponents without using the governing equations at all. Frontier 1 fixed the similarity-variable rate at γ=1/2 by dominant-balance power counting; take that as given and treat (T−t)^(1/2) as the problem's one length scale and (T−t) as its one time scale. Converting each field's own physical dimensions (above) into a power of (T−t) on that basis gives u1\~(T−t)^−1, ω1\~(T−t)^−3/2, ψ1\~(T−t)^−1/2 — matching p, q, s exactly — and, as a bonus this method adds, ν1 and ν2 come out at power 0: genuinely constant, not rescaling with (T−t) at all. That last point is a real constraint, not a formality: it is precisely the "constant viscosity" condition in Hou's own published description of his construction (Literature Context, above), now reached by an independent route — dimensional analysis, rather than dominant balance or Hou's own numerics — all three agreeing.
